Scaling: where the optimum sits

A fixed budget C = 6ND forces a choice between parameters and tokens. The exponents decide it — and two published fits of the same experiments decide it differently.

Start here

Chinchilla, in plain words

  1. You have one budget and two things to spend it on. Training cost is roughly C = 6ND — six, times the number of parameters N, times the number of training tokens D. Fix the budget and you have exactly one dial: a bigger model on less text, or a smaller model on more text.
  2. Until 2022 everyone bought parameters. GPT-3 was 175 billion parameters trained on 300 billion tokens — under two tokens of text per parameter.
  3. Chinchilla's claim was that the split was wrong. Same budget, spent differently: 70 billion parameters on 1.4 trillion tokens — twenty tokens per parameter. The smaller model won. That is where "20 tokens per parameter" comes from.
  4. Where the number comes from. Fit a curve for how the loss falls as you add parameters (rate α) and as you add tokens (rate β), then minimise it subject to the budget. The winning ratio is decided by α against β. If they are equal, tokens-per-parameter is a constant — a rule of thumb. If they differ, the ratio drifts as the budget grows, and there is no rule of thumb at all.
  5. Here is the part nobody mentions. The constants printed in the Chinchilla paper give α = 0.34, β = 0.28 — not equal. Put them through the algebra and they do not produce 20:1, and they do not reproduce the 70B model the paper is named after. A 2024 replication re-fit the same data and got α ≈ β, which does. Both fits are below. Switch between them and watch the recommendation move.

The vertical axis

What "held-out loss" means

Take text the model has never seen. Walk through it one token at a time. At each position the model holds a probability distribution over the whole vocabulary; look up the probability it gave to the token that actually came next, take the log, average over all positions, flip the sign:

L = −(1/T) Σt log pθ(xt | x<t)

Average surprise per token, in nats. Lower means the text was less surprising. Exponentiate it and you get perplexity — the effective number of equally-likely words the model is wavering between. Every loss on this page carries its perplexity next to it, because the perplexity is the one you can picture.

This is the log score: strictly proper, uniquely minimised at the true conditional. It is the same scoring rule you would demand of any probability you intend to act on — here applied to next-token prediction.

Which fit

Two published estimates of the same experiments

The replication reports that the printed Approach-3 estimates are inconsistent with Hoffmann's own first two estimation methods, fit the reconstructed data poorly, and carry confidence intervals far too tight for the number of runs behind them. Part of the cause is arithmetic housekeeping: the constants in the paper body are rounded relative to the TeX source, and the rounding biases the prediction.

The thing to look at

Does each fit reproduce the model it came from?

Both fits, run at the budget Chinchilla itself was trained on — C = 6 × 70B × 1.4T = 5.88e23 — against what DeepMind actually built.

A fit of an experiment that cannot recover the experiment's own headline result is a fitted response surface behaving badly — not a law of nature. That is the entire lesson, and it is checkable in four lines of algebra.

Predict first. Raise α and the loss falls faster with N — every extra parameter buys more. So: at a fixed budget, does the compute-optimal model get bigger or smaller? Commit before you touch the slider. (Most people answer this one wrong.)

Debrief — and the answer is smaller. The intuition that fails is reading α as how much parameters help. It is not: α is the rate at which their help decays. A steeper curve reaches its flat part sooner, so the marginal FLOP is better spent on tokens and N* falls. From the closed form ln N* = [ln(αA/βB) + β·ln(C/6)]/(α+β), ∂ln N*/∂α = (1/α − ln N*)/(α+β), which is negative for any N* > e1/α — about 19 parameters at the Chinchilla α. So for every model anyone has ever trained, raising α shrinks the optimum. The elasticity β/(α+β) falls too, so both move down; and in the right-hand panel the same fact appears as the optimal ray tilting — its slope is α/β, the 45° line only when α = β. Push the sliders far from the fitted region and the ratios become absurd. That is the honest reading of a regression evaluated outside its support, not a discovery.

Epistemic status: exact arithmetic on the declared parametric form L = E + A/Nα + B/Dβ, under two published sets of constants. Each optimum is computed in closed form and cross-checked against an independent numerical minimum on every redraw; a ⚠ next to the loss means the two disagreed. Nothing here is trained. This is the response surface, not a measurement of one. The two fits carry different irreducible floors E, so loss levels are not comparable between them — the allocation they recommend is.
